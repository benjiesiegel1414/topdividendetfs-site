What $QYLD Actually Is

$QYLD is the Global X Nasdaq 100 Covered Call ETF. It launched on December 11, 2013, and it's the fund that made covered call income mainstream for a lot of dividend investors, me included when I was first figuring out this space.

The strategy is simple. $QYLD owns the Nasdaq-100 stocks (Apple, NVIDIA, Microsoft and the rest, 102 holdings as of November 5, 2026) and sells call options on the index every month. The premium from those calls is what funds the monthly check. It tracks the Cboe Nasdaq-100 BuyWrite Index, manages about $7.74 billion, and charges a 0.60% expense ratio.

Here's the part most people miss: $QYLD sells calls on the whole portfolio, at the money. That's why the payout is so steady. It's also why the fund gives up almost all of the upside when tech rips. Keep that in mind, because it shows up in every number below.

How the QYLD Payout Cycle Works

$QYLD pays once a month, and the rhythm is tied to the monthly options cycle. In 2026 the ex-date has landed on the Monday after the third Friday of the month, right after the old call options expire and new ones get sold. The only exception was January, which went ex on Tuesday the 20th because of the Martin Luther King Jr. Day holiday. The record date is the same day as the ex-date, and the cash shows up three business days later, usually on Thursday.

November is the one exception on the pay side: the ex-date is Monday the 23rd, but Thanksgiving falls on Thursday the 26th, so the payment moves to Friday the 27th.

The best thing about this fund for planning purposes? Global X publishes the whole year's calendar ahead of time. You know the date months out. You just don't know the exact amount until a few days before.

The QYLD Yield: Which 13% Number?

Global X lists a 13.16% distribution rate as of November 5, 2026. That takes the latest monthly check, multiplies it by 12 and divides by NAV. The trailing 12-month distribution is 13.44%. Our live list shows about 12.4%. They're all measuring the same thing from slightly different angles, and the decimals don't matter much.

The one that looks scary is the 30-day SEC yield, which was -0.02%. That's not a mistake. The SEC yield only counts dividends and interest from the stocks minus expenses. Nasdaq-100 stocks pay tiny dividends, and the option premium doesn't count. So almost all of the 13% comes from selling calls.

Global X also says the distributions are estimated to include return of capital. ROC isn't automatically bad, since it lowers your cost basis instead of being taxed right away, but it's worth understanding. Check the 19a-1 notices and your 1099 for the real tax character. I broke down how ROC works in this return of capital explainer.

The Risks I'd Want You to Know

  • Capped upside. Selling at-the-money calls on the whole portfolio means $QYLD gives up most of the gains in a strong tech rally but still takes the losses when the Nasdaq falls.
  • Price decay. Our list flags it. Over long stretches the share price has tended to drift lower, so spending every check can mean slowly eating into your principal.
  • Concentration. The Nasdaq-100 is heavy in a handful of mega-cap tech names. Apple and NVIDIA alone were over 16% of the fund on November 5, 2026.
  • Return of capital. Global X estimates part of the distributions are ROC. Track your cost basis.
  • Not guaranteed. The dates are published, but the amount can change any month. It has been steady, not fixed.

My Takeaway

$QYLD's next ex-dividend date is November 23, 2026, paying November 27, and December goes ex on the 21st and pays the 24th. In 2026 it has paid 10 checks worth $1.79 per share, every one between $0.17 and $0.19, and $10,000 is producing roughly $106 to $108 a month.

If you want a predictable monthly check from the Nasdaq-100, $QYLD does exactly what it says. What it doesn't do is grow. I'd rather own something like $JEPQ for the core of a Nasdaq income position because it keeps more upside, and I'd only use $QYLD if I truly needed the steadiest possible check and understood the price decay going in. Either way, decide on purpose whether you're reinvesting or spending, and check total return once a quarter.
