Same Index, Two Very Different Machines

$JEPQ is the JPMorgan Nasdaq Equity Premium Income ETF, launched May 3, 2022. It's the giant of the group, with about $42.5 billion in assets. $QQQI is the NEOS Nasdaq-100 High Income ETF, launched January 29, 2024, and it has grown to $15.1 billion in net assets (NEOS, as of November 13, 2026) in under three years.

Both want the same thing: Nasdaq-100 stock exposure plus a big monthly check from selling call options. How they get there is different.

$JEPQ is actively managed. J.P. Morgan holds roughly 110 positions, mostly Nasdaq-100 stocks, and earns its option income through equity-linked notes (ELNs), which are notes issued by banks that pay out the premium from selling Nasdaq-100 calls. $QQQI owns the Nasdaq-100 stocks directly and sells (and sometimes buys) NDX index call options itself. Index options are Section 1256 contracts, and that one detail drives the biggest difference between these two funds: taxes. More on that below.

Here's the part most people miss: both funds are selling the same thing, Nasdaq-100 volatility. The yield gap comes from how much upside each one gives up and how the income is packaged, not from some secret edge. $JEPQ is the cheaper, actively managed version. $QQQI is the higher payout, tax-focused version.

The Tax Difference (This Is the Big One)

If you hold these in an IRA or Roth, skip this section. Taxes don't matter there, and the decision comes down to yield, fees and how steady you want the check.

In a taxable brokerage account, it's a different story. Most of $JEPQ's payout comes from ELN income, which is generally taxed as ordinary income at your full bracket. Only a small slice from stock dividends may qualify for the lower rate.

$QQQI's options are index options, which are Section 1256 contracts taxed 60% long term and 40% short term no matter how long the fund held them. On top of that, NEOS says $QQQI's distributions so far have been classified as return of capital, which isn't taxed when you receive it but lowers your cost basis, so you pay capital gains later when you sell. I broke this down in why NEOS ETFs are tax friendly.

So in a taxable account, $QQQI's after-tax income gap over $JEPQ is often wider than the headline yields suggest. Check your own 1099s and talk to a tax pro, because ROC classifications are estimated during the year and finalized after.

Which One I'd Rather Own

  • Pick $JEPQ if you want the lowest fee (0.35%), the biggest and most liquid fund, and you're holding it in an IRA or Roth where the ordinary income tax doesn't bite. You also get a bit more share price upside.
  • Pick $QQQI if you want the most income per dollar, a steadier monthly check, and you're investing in a taxable account where the 60/40 and return of capital treatment helps.
  • Own both if you want two paydays a month. $QQQI pays around the third week, $JEPQ at the start of the month. A 50/50 split has paid roughly 14% over the last year.

Personally, I care most about what lands in my account after taxes and whether the share price holds up. When I was building toward financial freedom, the funds I leaned on were the ones I could hold through a bad tech month without flinching. For a taxable account, that tilts me toward $QQQI. For a Roth, I'd happily take $JEPQ's lower fee. If you'd rather spread out beyond tech, I compared $QQQI with its S&P 500 sibling in $SPYI vs $QQQI.

The Risks I'd Want You to Know

  • Both lag $QQQ in strong markets. Selling calls trades upside for income. Over the window above, $QQQ beat both by several percentage points.
  • It's a tech bet. When the Nasdaq-100 drops, both drop with it. Both are down from their June 2026 highs.
  • Neither payout is fixed. $JEPQ's check has swung by more than 2x since 2024. $QQQI's has been steadier, but it can be cut too.
  • ELN counterparty risk ($JEPQ). ELNs are issued by banks. If an issuing bank ran into trouble, the note could lose value. J.P. Morgan spreads this across multiple counterparties, but it's a real, if small, risk.
  • Return of capital cuts both ways ($QQQI). It defers tax and lowers your cost basis, so you'll owe more in capital gains when you sell.
  • Fees. $68 a year on $10,000 for $QQQI versus $35 for $JEPQ. Small, but it compounds.

My Takeaway

$QQQI pays more (about 15% versus 12.7%) with a steadier check and a tax-friendlier setup. $JEPQ is cheaper, bigger and has held its price slightly better. Taxable account and want max income: I lean $QQQI. Retirement account and want low cost: $JEPQ is hard to beat. Either way, track total return, not just yield. Frequently Asked Questions: JEPQ vs QQQI

  • Is $JEPQ or $QQQI better?+
    It depends on your account and goals. $QQQI pays more, about 15.2% over the last 12 months versus about 12.7% for $JEPQ, with a steadier monthly check and Section 1256 option income that NEOS has classified as return of capital. $JEPQ charges about half the fee (0.35% vs 0.68%) and held its price slightly better. From January 30, 2024 to November 16, 2026, $10,000 with the cash taken out grew to about $14,135 in $QQQI and $13,905 in $JEPQ.
  • Which pays more, $JEPQ or $QQQI?+
    $QQQI. Its last 12 monthly payouts (through November 2026) total $7.5843 per share, about 15.2% of its $49.91 close on November 16, 2026. $JEPQ's last 12 total $6.94823 per share, about 12.7% of its $54.86 close. On $10,000 that is roughly $1,520 a year from $QQQI versus $1,267 from $JEPQ, before taxes.
  • Is $QQQI more tax efficient than $JEPQ?+
    Usually, in a taxable account. $JEPQ's income comes mostly from equity-linked notes and is generally taxed as ordinary income. $QQQI uses index options taxed under Section 1256 (60% long term, 40% short term), and NEOS says its distributions so far have been classified as return of capital, which lowers your cost basis instead of being taxed right away. In an IRA or Roth the difference does not matter.
  • Do $JEPQ and $QQQI pay on the same day?+
    No. $JEPQ usually goes ex-dividend on the first business day of the month and pays about two business days later, plus a second late-December distribution paid in early January. $QQQI follows the NEOS calendar, with its November 2026 distribution ($0.5838) going ex on November 18 and paying November 20, and December going ex on December 16. Owning both gives you two monthly paydays.
  • Can you own both $JEPQ and $QQQI?+
    Yes. Their top holdings overlap heavily (Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Micron and AMD are top names in both), so you are not diversifying much, but a 50/50 split has paid roughly 14% over the last year and gives you two paydays a month. Many investors hold $JEPQ in retirement accounts and $QQQI in taxable accounts.
