Why 2,000 Shares?

I get asked all the time how many shares of $SCHD is "enough." People usually want a dollar figure. I think a share count is a better target, and here's why.

Your dividend income is just shares times dividend per share. You control the first number. The fund controls the second. Once you own the shares, every dividend raise goes straight to you without you adding another dollar. So the game is simple: lock in a meaningful share count while you're young, then let time work on the dividend per share.

2,000 is the number I like because it's big enough to matter and small enough to be reachable. At $34.39 a share (November 23, 2026 close), 2,000 shares costs about $68,780. That's a real stretch in your 20s. It's also something a lot of people can get to in five to ten years of steady buying. And as you'll see below, 2,000 shares plus a long runway is where the math starts getting interesting.

Today those 2,000 shares pay about $2,108 a year, roughly $527 a quarter. Nobody is retiring on that. The whole idea rests on what happens to that $2,108 over 25, 30 or 35 years.

What $SCHD Actually Is

$SCHD is the Schwab U.S. Dividend Equity ETF. It launched on October 20, 2011 and tracks the Dow Jones U.S. Dividend 100 Index. In plain English, it owns about 100 U.S. companies that have paid dividends for at least 10 straight years and that score well on cash flow, return on equity, yield and dividend growth.

It holds about $118 billion, charges a 0.06% expense ratio (that's $6 a year per $10,000), and pays quarterly, in March, June, September and December. The share count math in this post is split-adjusted, since $SCHD did a 3-for-1 split in October 2024.

The reason this fund works for a "buy shares and wait" plan is that it isn't chasing the highest yield. It's built around companies that can keep raising the payout. If you want the full list of what's inside, I keep the $SCHD holdings updated, and every quarterly payment is on my $SCHD dividend history page.

Here's the part most people miss. The dividend didn't just go up. It went up every single calendar year since the fund's first full year, through 2018's selloff, through 2020 and through 2022's bear market. The size of the raise bounced around a lot, from under 4% in 2023 to almost 20% in 2019, but the direction never changed.

Put it together and the dividend compounded at about 11.0% a year from 2012 to 2025. That's the number the whole 2,000 share idea leans on, so I want to be straight about it: the pace has cooled. Over the last five years it was about 9.2% a year. Over the last three it was about 7.1%. A fund that's grown to $118 billion probably can't keep raising its payout 11% a year forever. I'll come back to that, because it changes the answer a lot.

The Backtest: 2,000 Shares Bought at Launch

Let's make it real. Say someone bought 2,000 (split-adjusted) shares the day $SCHD started trading, October 20, 2011. The closing price that day was $8.33 split-adjusted, so the whole position cost about $16,654.

If they never bought another share and spent every dividend: the position paid $540 in 2012 and $2,095 in 2025. They've collected about $18,498 in total dividends through September 2026, which is more than the entire original cost. The shares are worth about $68,780 today. And the dividend alone now pays about 12.7% a year on what they originally put in.

If they reinvested every dividend: 2,000 shares became about 3,215 shares without adding a dime. That position is worth about $110,579 today and paid about $3,210 in 2025. At the current payout rate it's throwing off about $3,389 a year, which is over 20% a year on the original $16,654.

That's 15 years. The 2,000 share idea is about what happens when you give it 30 or more.

Look at how wide that range is. If the full 2012 to 2025 pace repeated for three decades and you reinvested everything, 2,000 shares would pay about $119,386 a year. Even after 30 years of 3% inflation that's about $49,186 in today's money. That's a retirement paycheck for a lot of people, from a position that costs about $68,780 today.

Now look at the 6% row. Same shares, same 30 years, and it pays about $29,953 a year, or about $12,340 in today's dollars. That's a great supplement. It's not a retirement on its own.

So here's what I actually think. The 11% row is the "everything goes right" case, and I wouldn't plan my life around it. But even the middle rows are a big deal for a position you could finish building in your 20s or early 30s. And notice what reinvesting does: in every row it more than doubles the income, because you end year 30 with about 4,947 shares instead of 2,000. You bought 2,000. The dividends bought the rest.

This is the whole reason the title says ASAP. Compare the person who gets to 2,000 shares at 25 with the person who gets there at 35. Same shares. Same fund. Same retirement age. The 25 year old ends up with roughly two to four times the income, depending on the growth rate, purely because of ten extra years of compounding.

You can't buy those years back later. At 40 you can absolutely still do this, and the numbers are still good. You just need more shares to land in the same place. When I was building my own income, this was the part that changed how I thought about it. Getting the shares early mattered more than getting the perfect price.

A few things I'd do if I were starting this from zero today:

Automate it. Pick an amount, set it to buy every paycheck or every month, and stop looking at the price. The share count is the scoreboard, not the account value.

Turn on DRIP from day one. In the backtest above, reinvesting alone added about 1,215 shares over 15 years. Those are shares you never had to pay for.

Use a Roth IRA if you can. Dividends reinvested inside a Roth aren't taxed along the way, which is the assumption all the reinvesting math above quietly makes. In a regular taxable account you'll owe tax on the dividends each year, and that slows the compounding. Talk to a tax professional about your own situation.

Treat down years as a sale. If the price drops 20%, your monthly buy gets you more shares. That's the entire point of chasing a share count instead of a dollar figure.

What Could Go Wrong

  • Dividend growth slows. This is the big one, and it's already happening: 11.0% a year since 2012, 9.2% over the last five, 7.1% over the last three. The 30-year table shows how much that single number changes the outcome.
  • The dividend can be cut. $SCHD has never had a down calendar year, but it's only been around since 2011. It has never been through something like 2008, when a lot of dividend payers slashed payouts.
  • The price can fall a long way. It dropped in 2018 and 2022 and went basically nowhere from the end of 2021 to the end of 2025. You need to be able to hold through years like that, and any investment can theoretically go to zero.
  • Inflation eats the big number. $119,000 in 30 years is not $119,000 today. Always look at the "today's dollars" column.
  • Taxes. Outside a retirement account, dividends are taxed every year, so real-world reinvesting compounds slower than the math here.
  • The index can change. $SCHD follows an index with rules, and those rules or the fund itself could change over 30 years.
  • It's one fund. I like $SCHD a lot, but I wouldn't make any single ETF my entire plan. I wrote about who it does and doesn't fit in $SCHD is not good for most people.

My Takeaway

2,000 shares of $SCHD is not a magic number and it's not a guarantee of anything. It's a target that's concrete, reachable in your 20s or 30s, and big enough that decades of dividend growth can turn it into something meaningful.

The real data says a 2,000 share position bought at launch went from $540 a year to $2,095 a year in 13 years without adding a dollar, and to about $3,389 a year with reinvesting. The math says that if growth keeps anything close to that pace, the same position could be paying tens of thousands a year by the time a 25 or 30 year old retires. And if growth comes in at half the historical rate, it's still a very nice second paycheck.

I'd rather own the shares early and be wrong about the growth rate than wait for certainty and lose the years. That's the idea. It's just an idea, so run your own numbers in the calculator above and decide for yourself.
