What Happened Today

On November 5, 2026, $QQQI fell about 5.7% in one day, from $51.82 to $48.88, as the Nasdaq-100 ($QQQ) dropped about 6.5% ($691.95 to $647.05). That puts $QQQI about 15% below its June 2, 2026 high of $57.69, and about 10% below where it closed on October 28 ($54.12).

Notice that $QQQI fell less than $QQQ. That's the option premium doing part of its job as a cushion. But a cushion isn't a wall, and the question I keep getting is the same one: "Does this mean my monthly check is about to get cut?"

I pulled every $QQQI distribution since it launched on January 29, 2024 and lined each one up with the share price right before its ex-date. The answer is pretty clear.

How the Option Strategy Works

Why the payout behaves this way, in plain English.

According to NEOS, $QQQI holds the stocks in the Nasdaq-100 and runs a "data-driven call option strategy that may include both sold and purchased NDX index options," which "may provide the opportunity for upside capture in rising equity markets." NEOS doesn't publish the exact strikes or expirations it uses, so the example below is a simple, generic illustration, not $QQQI's actual trades.

Say the Nasdaq-100 index is at 20,000 and the fund sells a call option with a strike slightly above that. The buyer pays the fund a premium up front. Then one of three things happens:

Scenario 1

The index falls below the strike

The sold calls expire worthless and the fund keeps the full premium. But the premium only cushions part of the drop.

Result: the price falls, and next month's dollar payout tends to shrink with it.

Scenario 2

The index ends near or slightly above the strike

The fund keeps most of the premium and gets the stock gains up to the strike.

Result: this is the sweet spot for a covered call fund.

Scenario 3

The index rips well past the strike

The sold calls cost the fund money and upside gets capped. Any calls NEOS buys can win back some of that upside.

Result: you still make money, just less than $QQQ.

Why selloffs aren't all bad for the premium. When markets fall hard, option prices (implied volatility) usually rise. Each new call the fund sells brings in more premium per dollar, which helps $QQQI keep paying about 14% of a lower price. The flip side: selling calls at lower index levels means less room before upside gets capped if the market snaps back fast.

Why the payout scales with the fund's size. $QQQI's 30-day SEC yield was about -0.05% as of September 30, 2026. In other words, the dividends from the Nasdaq-100 stocks don't even cover the 0.68% expense ratio. The money you get each month comes from option premiums (and has been classified as return of capital), and option premium is earned on the fund's assets. Smaller assets per share means a smaller check per share.

Taxes. The NDX index options are taxed as Section 1256 contracts (60% long-term, 40% short-term). NEOS also uses tax-loss harvesting, and distributions have been classified as return of capital based on estimates; the final breakdown comes on Form 1099-DIV. A tax pro can tell you what that means for your account. If you want the full list of things that can go wrong, read our breakdown of $QQQI's biggest risks.

Should You Worry?

  • Your dollar income will likely dip. If the price stays lower, expect smaller checks. That's what the data says, and it's what happened in April 2025.
  • The yield has held. In 33 months the payout never dropped below 1.14% of the price. A lower check on a lower price is not the same thing as a "cut" in the strategy.
  • Total return still depends on the Nasdaq. $QQQI owns the Nasdaq-100. Its NAV total return over the past year was 7.09% versus 10.47% for the index, so it isn't immune to tech selloffs. It just softens them a bit and gives up some upside.
  • Reinvesting at lower prices buys more shares. If you reinvest, a lower price means each check buys more shares, which can lift future income when the price recovers.
  • The real risk is a long slump. If the Nasdaq stays down for a long time, the price and the payout could stay lower, and a fund paying out about 14% a year could see NAV erosion. So far it hasn't: the price recovered after April 2025 and set new highs in 2026.

My Takeaway

A 5.7% drop in one day stings. I get it. But I don't look at $QQQI as "$0.63 a month forever." I look at it as about 14% a year of whatever the fund is worth. The data backs that up across all 33 payouts, through the April 2025 crash and back.

So yes, if the price stays near $48.88, I'd expect a smaller check in November. If the Nasdaq bounces, I'd expect the check to bounce with it, just like it did in May 2025. The thing that would actually worry me is a long, grinding Nasdaq bear market, because then the price and the payout both stay lower. That's the trade-off you accept for a 14% yield on tech.
