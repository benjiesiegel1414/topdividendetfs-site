What Is Simply Wall St?

Simply Wall St is a visual stock research platform. Instead of handing you a wall of numbers from an annual report, it turns a company's financial data into simple charts and plain-English checks you can read in a few minutes.

The thing it's best known for is the Snowflake. Every company gets a five-sided shape that scores it on:

Snowflake scoreWhat it tells you
ValueWhether the stock looks cheap or expensive based on its fundamentals
Future GrowthHow analysts expect earnings and revenue to grow
Past PerformanceHow the business has actually performed over recent years
Financial HealthDebt, cash and the strength of the balance sheet
DividendsHow attractive and how reliable the dividend looks

A big, full Snowflake means a company scores well across the board. A lopsided one shows you at a glance where the weak spot is. For a dividend investor, a stock with a fat Dividends score and a tiny Financial Health score is a yellow flag worth digging into before you buy.

It covers more than 120,000 stocks, ETFs and funds across 90+ markets, so it works whether you hold US dividend stocks, international payers or a mix.

How Dividend Investors Can Use Simply Wall St

Here's where it gets practical. These are the four ways I think it earns its spot for income investors.

1. Checking dividend safety before you buy

This is the part most people skip, and it's the part that matters most. A high yield means nothing if the dividend gets cut six months after you buy.

Every dividend stock on Simply Wall St has a dividend section that lays out:

  • Dividend yield, compared with the industry average
  • Payout ratio, so you can see how much of earnings is going out the door
  • Earnings coverage, a plain check on whether profits cover the dividend
  • Cash flow coverage using the cash payout ratio, which I like even more because dividends are paid in cash, not accounting earnings
  • Dividend history, with checks for whether the payout has been stable and growing over the last 10 years
  • Upcoming dividend dates and a forecast dividend yield when they're available

That's basically the checklist I run on any dividend stock, laid out on one page. If you're newer to this, my dividend investing for beginners guide explains why payout ratio and coverage matter so much.

💡 Works for what's inside your ETFs, too

A fund like $SCHD or $VYM is only as strong as the companies it holds. Pull up the top 10 holdings and run them through the dividend checks above. It's a fast way to see whether your fund's income is built on solid ground. Our SCHD vs VYM comparison is a good place to start.

2. Tracking your portfolio and dividend income in one place

Simply Wall St has a portfolio tracker with a built-in dividend tracker. It calculates your annual and monthly dividend income, current yield and yield on cost, and shows your dividend payments over time. Yield on cost is one of my favorite numbers to watch, because it's how you see years of dividend raises actually paying off.

You can get holdings in by syncing a brokerage, uploading a spreadsheet or statement, or adding them by hand. If you have money spread across several accounts, I also wrote about tracking every dividend from every brokerage in one place.

3. Screeners to find dividend stocks that fit your rules

The stock screener lets you filter by the Snowflake scores, including the Dividends score, and see dividend yield and forecast dividend yield right in the results. There are also ready-made investing ideas, like one for dividend stocks yielding over 3%, if you'd rather start from a list than build your own filters.

My tip: don't sort by highest yield and call it a day. Start with a decent Dividends score and a healthy balance sheet, then look at yield. That order alone keeps you out of a lot of yield traps.

4. Watchlists and Narratives for the companies you own

Watchlists let you keep tabs on companies you own or want to own. Narratives go a step further. You write down your thesis for a stock (why you own it and what you think it's worth), set a fair value, and track it over time. You can also read other investors' narratives and the fair values they've set.

I love this for dividend investing. Writing down why you bought something is the best defense against panic-selling a good company on a bad week.

Who Simply Wall St Is Best For (and Who Might Not Need It)

It's a great fit if you are:

  • A beginner who learns visually. The Snowflake and the plain-English checks make financial statements far less intimidating.
  • A long-term dividend investor who owns individual stocks and wants a quick safety check before adding to a position.
  • Anyone who wants solid research without reading 10-Ks. You get the key numbers without digging through hundreds of pages of filings.

You might not need it if:

  • You only own one or two broad index or dividend ETFs and rarely trade. You don't need stock-level research to hold a fund.
