Why "How Often Does SCHD Rebalance" Is a Confusing Question

A lot of people ask this because they saw a big shake-up in $SCHD's top holdings and wondered what happened. The confusing part is that two different things get called a "rebalance."

One is the quarterly reweighting. Same 100 stocks, new weights. The other is the annual reconstitution, which is the only time stocks actually get kicked out and new ones come in. Mix those up and the whole schedule looks random.

I've tracked every one of these on TopDividendETFs.com this year, so here's the plain-English version of how it works, with the real 2026 dates. If you just want the holdings themselves, the full list lives on our $SCHD holdings for 2026 page.

The short version

Four times a year, $SCHD trims its winners and tops up its laggards so no stock is above about 4% and no sector above 25%. Once a year, in March, it re-screens the whole market and swaps out stocks that no longer make the cut. That's it.

The Quarterly Rebalance: Same Stocks, New Weights

What happens every March, June, September and December

$SCHD tracks the Dow Jones U.S. Dividend 100 Index. Per the S&P Dow Jones Indices methodology, the index's stocks are weighted quarterly using a capped float-adjusted market cap approach. Bigger companies get bigger weights, but with two hard limits:

  • No single stock above 4.0%
  • No sector above 25%

Anything over the cap gets trimmed and the extra weight gets spread across the rest. Between rebalances the weights just drift with stock prices, so a stock that runs hard can float above 4% until the next reset.

Here's a real example. In the September 2026 rebalance, which took effect September 21, $QCOM jumped from 2.68% to 4.74% and became $SCHD's #1 holding, $TXN went from 3.22% to 4.41%, and $MRK was trimmed by 0.80 points. Zero stocks were added or removed. I broke down every move in the $SCHD Q3 2026 rebalance post.

There's also a safety valve. The methodology runs a daily check, and if the stocks weighing more than 4.7% add up to more than 22% of the index, it reweights early using the same quarterly method. That's rare, but it means the caps can't drift too far.

The Annual Reconstitution: The Only Time Stocks Change

Every March, the whole index gets re-screened

This is the big one. Once a year the index starts over from the Dow Jones U.S. Broad Stock Market Index and runs its screens again. To even be considered, a stock needs:

  • At least 10 consecutive years of dividend payments
  • A float-adjusted market cap of at least $500 million
  • At least $2 million in average daily trading value over three months
  • Not a REIT (REITs are excluded)

Then only the top half by dividend yield moves on. Those stocks get ranked on four things: free cash flow to total debt, return on equity, dividend yield and 5-year dividend growth. Add the four ranks together and the best 100 composite scores make the index, with a buffer that lets existing holdings stay as long as they rank in the top 200.

The data snapshot is taken before March. Dividend history is measured as of the last business day of December, and market cap, liquidity and yield as of the third Friday of February. The changes then take effect at the open on the Monday after the third Friday of March.

The 2026 reconstitution was a big one: 25 stocks added and 22 removed, effective March 23, 2026. Energy names like $VLO and $HAL went out, and $UNH, $ABT, $PG and $QCOM came in. Every name is listed on our $SCHD 2026 reconstitution page.

What a Rebalance Means for You as a Holder

Short answer: you don't have to do anything. The fund handles all the trading. You don't get a notice, you don't vote, and your share count doesn't change.

Here's what I actually pay attention to. A quarterly reweight barely matters to me. It's housekeeping. The March reconstitution is where I look closer, because it can change the fund's character. When 25 stocks rotate in, the sector mix, the yield and even the dividend growth rate going forward can move. If the new lineup leans more into healthcare and tech and less into energy, that's a different fund than it was a year earlier, even with the same ticker.

This is the part most people miss: $SCHD's rebalance months are also its dividend months. It pays quarterly, with ex-dates in March, June, September and December. The two events are separate, but that's why rebalance news and dividend news tend to land in the same week. All the payout dates are on the $SCHD 2026 dividend dates page, and if you want to see what your shares pay, try the $SCHD dividend calculator.

The Risks and Trade-Offs of This Rules-Based Approach

  • It's backward looking. The screens use last year's numbers. A company can look great in February data and cut its dividend in June, and it stays in until the next March review unless S&P steps in.
  • Turnover can be lumpy. Some years barely change. 2026 swapped about a quarter of the index. Big turnover can shift the yield and sector exposure in one shot.
  • The caps cut both ways. The 4% stock cap keeps you diversified, but it also means $SCHD trims its biggest winners every quarter.
  • No REITs, by design. If you want real estate income, you'll need a separate fund.

My Takeaway

So how often does $SCHD rebalance? Weights reset four times a year, in March, June, September and December. Stocks only change once a year, in March. The next reweight lands after the close on December 18, 2026, and the next real shake-up comes in March 2027.

Honestly, I like this setup. It's boring, rules-based and predictable. You know the dates in advance, you know the caps, and you know what the screens look for: cash flow, profitability, yield and dividend growth. That discipline is a big reason $SCHD sits at about $116 billion in assets with no price decay on our list.
