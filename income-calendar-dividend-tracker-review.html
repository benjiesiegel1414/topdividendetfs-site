Why I care about a forward-looking dividend calendar

Most brokerage apps are built to show you what already happened. You can see the dividend that hit your account last Tuesday, but it is a lot harder to answer the questions that actually matter when you live off your portfolio: How much is coming next month? Which months are going to be light? When is the next ex-dividend date I need to own the shares by?

For a lot of dividend investors, the usual answer to those questions is a spreadsheet. It works, until you own a mix of monthly payers, quarterly payers and option income ETFs whose payouts change every month. Then the spreadsheet turns into a part-time job.

That is the gap Income Calendar is built to fill. Instead of tracking what you were paid, it projects what you are about to be paid, and it puts every declaration, ex-dividend date and pay date on an actual calendar.

Who makes Income Calendar (you probably already know them)

Income Calendar comes from BNK Invest, the same team behind DividendChannel.com and its DRIP Returns Calculator. If you have ever plugged a ticker into that calculator to see how reinvesting dividends would have grown an investment over time, you have already used their work. It is one of the most popular free tools in the dividend investing space, and it has been around for years.

In my opinion, these guys make top-tier tools for dividend investors. They have been building dividend data tools for a long time, and Income Calendar takes that same data and points it forward: instead of looking at what reinvesting did in the past, it shows you what your portfolio is projected to pay next. It even has a DRIP mode, so you can see how reinvesting changes your future income.

Step 1: Enter your holdings in the free preview

Head to the free preview page, type a ticker and a share count, and hit Add holding. You can also tick a DRIP box for any position you reinvest. Once you have your holdings in, you click Generate 12-Month Projection and the full Income Calendar demo opens with your portfolio loaded.

If you do not want to type anything, there is also a View Demo Portfolio button that loads a pre-built portfolio of Dow stocks so you can click around right away. Setup on the paid version is just as quick: you can import from your brokerage or enter positions by hand, and the company says most people see a full 12-month projection in about 7 minutes.

Step 2: The 12-month projected income view

This is the first screen you see, and honestly it is the one I keep coming back to. A bar chart of every month for the next year, with the exact projected dollar amount next to each month and a 12-month total at the bottom.

Two things jumped out at me right away:

  • December is the biggest month ($540.20) because the quarterly payers ($SCHD and $KO) land in the same month as the monthly payers, and $JEPI and $JEPQ are projected to pay twice in December (early December and again on December 31).
  • January is the lightest month ($168.45) for the exact same reason. Because $JEPI and $JEPQ's year-end payment lands on December 31, January has no payment from either of them.

That is the kind of thing you would never notice from a yield number. On paper, a 6.88% yield on about $65,000 sounds like roughly $375 a month. In reality, my monthly income ranges from about $168 to $540. If you pay bills from your dividends, knowing that January is going to be light before January shows up is the whole point.

Add up the top four and you get the real story: $JEPQ, $QQQI, $JEPI and $SPYI produce about 66% of this portfolio's income, even though they are 4 of 10 holdings and only about 41% of the portfolio's value. Those option income ETFs adjust their payouts every month, so most of this portfolio's income moves with the options market.

That is not good or bad by itself, but it is something you want to know. The company calls this spotting income concentration, and it is one of the most useful things the tool does.

Step 4: What is still coming this year

The Remaining 2026 view lists every projected payment left in the calendar year, in date order, with a subtotal for each month. When I ran it, there was $751.40 still projected for the rest of 2026: $211.20 still to come in November and $540.20 in December.

This is the screen I would use for year-end planning: what cash is coming before December 31, when it lands, and from which holding.

Step 5: The actual calendar (this is where it shines)

Click any month and you get a real calendar. The summary view shows which holdings have an event on each day, color-coded by type: declarations, ex-dividend dates, projected ex-dividend dates, payments and projected payments, plus earnings dates and market holidays.

Scroll down and the Details calendar spells out every event with the per-share amount. For example, it shows $KO going ex-dividend on December 1 for $0.53 per share, $SCHD's projected ex-dividend date on December 10, and the daily income total under each pay date.

Why the ex-dividend date matters: you have to own the shares before the ex-dividend date to get the next payment. Seeing projected ex-dates on a calendar makes it easy to plan buys, sells and rebalancing around them instead of finding out after the fact.

Step 6: The full-year grid and every month at once

The ALL view is my favorite for a quick health check. At the top is a grid with every holding as a row: shares, current value, next pay date, projected income for each of the next 12 months, the 12-month total and the annualized yield.

Below the grid it lays out all 12 monthly calendars back to back, so you can scroll through the whole year and see exactly how payments cluster.

Who Income Calendar is for

It is a great fit if you:

  • Live off your dividends, or plan to, and need to know which months are strong and which are light.
  • Own a mix of monthly and quarterly payers across more than one account.
  • Hold option income ETFs whose payouts change every month and want projections that update on their own.
  • Are tired of maintaining a dividend spreadsheet.

Keep in mind:

  • Income Calendar is not a trading platform. It does not place trades or make recommendations. It is a planning and visibility tool.
  • Projections are estimates. Dividends can change, especially for option income ETFs, and the tool updates as new dividend information comes in.

My take

What I like about Income Calendar is that it is built around the question every income investor actually asks: what is coming next, and when?

Running my test portfolio through it took a couple of minutes, and I learned three things I would not have spotted from a yield number: my January would be the lightest month by far, four option income ETFs were driving about two-thirds of the income, and $JEPI and $JEPQ's year-end payment shifts income from January into December. That is exactly the kind of planning information that helps when your dividends pay real bills.

Frequently asked questions

What is Income Calendar?

Income Calendar is a forward-looking dividend tracker from BNK Invest, the team behind DividendChannel.com and Contrarian Outlook. You add your holdings and it projects your dividend income for the next 12 months by month, by position and by date, including projected declaration, ex-dividend and pay dates.

How much does Income Calendar cost?

Income Calendar is $8.99 per month with one plan that includes every core feature: 12-month projections, unlimited holdings and accounts, brokerage import, manual entry and dividend alerts. You can cancel anytime.

Is there a free version of Income Calendar?

Yes. The free preview at DividendTracker.com lets you enter up to 10 holdings and see a temporary 12-month income projection. The preview does not need a login, a credit card or a brokerage connection, and nothing you enter is saved. The paid subscription does require an account and a card.

Does Income Calendar work with ETFs?

Yes. It supports U.S. and Canadian stocks, ETFs, closed-end funds, REITs, BDCs, preferreds and mutual funds. In my test it handled monthly payers like JEPI, JEPQ, QQQI and SPYI right next to quarterly payers like SCHD, KO and ABBV.

Are Income Calendar's projections guaranteed?

No. Projections are estimates based on recent dividends and historical payment patterns. Companies and funds can raise, cut, delay or cancel payouts, and high-income option ETFs change their distributions often. The tool updates as new dividend information comes in, but treat every projection as a planning estimate.
