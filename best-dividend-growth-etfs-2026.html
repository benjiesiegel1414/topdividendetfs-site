What Is a Dividend Growth ETF?

A dividend growth ETF owns companies that keep raising their dividends year after year, instead of the companies with the biggest yield today. Most of them follow a simple rule. $VIG wants 10 straight years of raises. $DGRO wants 5. $NOBL only owns S&P 500 companies that have raised their dividend for 25 years in a row, the famous Dividend Aristocrats.

The idea is that a company able to raise its dividend through recessions, rate hikes and bad years is usually a healthy, profitable business. You give up some yield on day one (many of these funds yield 1.5% to 3%), but the paycheck keeps getting bigger. Hold long enough and the income on what you originally paid can pass what a high yield fund would have paid you.

That is the whole game with these funds: you are buying a raise, not a big check today.

Dividend growth vs. high yield, in one example

$VYM, Vanguard's high dividend fund, yields about 2.2%, a hair below $SCHD's 2.9%. But since 2017, $VYM's payout grew 46% while $SCHD's grew 133%. Someone who bought each one 10 years ago now earns about 5.0% a year on cost from $VYM and 7.5% from $SCHD. Same starting point, very different paycheck.

Why Yield on Cost Matters

$SCHD yields about 2.9% if you buy it today. But someone who bought 10 years ago is collecting about $749 a year on every $10,000 they put in, a 7.5% yield on their original money, and that number goes up every time the fund raises its payout. On top of that, the $10,000 itself grew to about $35,907 with dividends reinvested.

Compare that with $SPY. The S&P 500 grew $10,000 into more ($40,816), but it only pays about $350 a year on the original $10,000. If your goal is a growing stream of cash you can live on, dividend growth funds do that job better. If your goal is the biggest possible balance, the plain S&P 500 has been hard to beat.

How to Pick a Dividend Growth ETF

The 5 things I look at

  • The raise record, not the yield. Did the payout go up in most years? $SCHD, $DGRO, $VIG and $VYM raised it all 5 of the last 5 years. $RDVY did it only 2 times.
  • The rule behind the fund. 25-year aristocrats ($NOBL) are the most proven, but the strict rule leaves out younger fast growers. 5 to 10 year rules ($DGRO, $VIG, $SCHD) catch more of them.
  • The starting yield. A 3% yield growing 9% a year gets you to a high yield on cost much faster than a 1% yield growing 10% a year.
  • The fee. $VIG (0.04%), $SCHD (0.06%) and $DGRO (0.08%) cost almost nothing. Several others charge 0.35% to 0.50%, which eats into every dividend.
  • Total return. A growing dividend is great, but the fund still has to grow your money. Check the 10-year return column before buying.

The Risks I'd Want You to Know

  • Past growth is not a promise. $RDVY's dividend fell about 28% from 2023 to 2025 and $DGRW's has been flat since 2022. Index changes and sector shifts can slow a fund's growth fast.
  • Lower yield today. If you need income right now, a 1.5% yield on $VIG will feel small. Dividend growth works best with time.
  • They can lag the market. Over the last 10 years every fund here returned less than $SPY. You are trading some total return for a steadier, rising paycheck.
  • Payouts are lumpy. Quarterly dividends move around, and year-end payouts can include one-time distributions, so look at full years, not single checks.

My Takeaway

If I could only own one dividend growth ETF, it would be $SCHD. It pays about 2.9% today, costs 0.06%, and more than doubled its payout from 2017 to 2025 without a single down year. $DGRO is my pick for the broadest, steadiest grower, and $VIG for the lowest fee and the strongest total return among the big three. $NOBL is great if you only want the 25-year aristocrats and don't mind the higher fee.

The bigger lesson: a fund yielding 3% today that raises its payout 9% a year will pay you about 7% a year on your original money within a decade, more than a typical high dividend fund like $VYM pays today. Buy early, reinvest, and let the raises do the work.

Frequently Asked Questions: Dividend Growth ETFs

  • What is a dividend growth ETF?+
    A dividend growth ETF is a fund that owns companies with a record of raising their dividends, instead of companies with the highest yield today. Most follow a rule, like 10 straight years of raises for $VIG or 25 years for $NOBL. The starting yield is usually lower, but the payout tends to grow faster than the market's.
  • What is the best dividend growth ETF?+
    By the numbers, $SCHD has the strongest mix of yield and growth among the big funds: about a 2.9% yield, a 0.06% expense ratio and a dividend that grew about 11% a year from 2017 to 2025. $DGRO and $VIG are close behind with lower yields and very low fees. The best one for you depends on whether you want more income now or more growth later.
  • Which dividend growth ETF has grown its dividend the fastest?+
    Among funds with a full record since 2017, $SCHD grew its annual payout the most, up 133% from $0.45 to $1.05 per share, about 11.2% a year. $REGL (10.3% a year) and $RDVY (9.6% a year) were next, but $RDVY's payout has fallen since 2023. Over the last five years, $VIGI, $DGRS, $SCHD and $VIG led, partly because 2020 was a weak starting point.
  • Is SCHD or VIG better for dividend growth?+
    $SCHD pays more today (2.92% vs 1.45%) and grew its dividend faster since 2017 (11.2% vs 8.0% a year). Over the last five years both grew about 9.2% a year. $VIG has had slightly better total return over 10 years (13.8% vs 13.6% a year) because it owns more fast-growing tech names. Both cost almost nothing to own.
  • Do dividend growth ETFs beat the S&P 500?+
    Not on total return over the last 10 years. $SPY returned about 15.1% a year, while the best dividend growth ETFs returned roughly 13.6% to 14.7% a year. Where they win is income growth: $SCHD's payout grew more than twice as fast as $SPY's since 2017.
  • What is yield on cost?+
    Yield on cost is your dividend income divided by what you originally paid, not today's price. If you bought $SCHD 10 years ago, the dividends it pays today equal about 7.5% of your purchase price a year, even though new buyers only get about 2.9%.
