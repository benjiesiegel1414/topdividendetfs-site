Why $GPIX Is Worth Knowing About

When people want monthly income from the S&P 500, they almost always land on $JEPI or $SPYI. $GPIX is Goldman Sachs' version, and it has quietly grown to about $6.3 billion in assets without getting nearly as much attention.

It owns the S&P 500 stocks, sells call options on part of the portfolio, and pays you once a month. The expense ratio is 0.29%, cheaper than both $JEPI (0.35%) and $SPYI (0.68%).

This page is the calendar: when $GPIX goes ex-dividend, when the cash hits your account, and how much every payment was. It's the S&P 500 sibling of $GPIQ, which I already broke down on the $GPIQ 2026 dividend dates page. Same issuer, same calendar, different index.

How to read the $GPIX schedule

Goldman declares the distribution on the last business day of the month. The very next business day is the ex-date, so you need to own $GPIX before the open on the first business day of the month to get that month's check. The cash usually lands four to seven days later.

That's the exact same rhythm as $GPIQ, and close to $JEPI, which also goes ex early in the month. $SPYI is different: NEOS usually goes ex in the second half of the month (its October payout went ex on October 21). If you own $GPIX and $SPYI together, your income shows up at two different points in the month. $DIVO is different again: it goes ex about a day before month-end and pays the next business day (see the $DIVO 2026 dividend dates).

$GPIX holds the S&P 500 stocks directly, led by $NVDA, $AAPL, $MSFT and $AMZN, so its top holdings look almost identical to $SPY. Then it writes call options on roughly 25% to 75% of the portfolio and pays the premium out as a monthly distribution. Goldman's stated goal is consistent income plus capital growth.

That range is the key. Because $GPIX doesn't write calls on the whole portfolio, it keeps more upside when the market rips. That's why its yield is lower than $SPYI's and why its total return has stayed close to the index. $JEPI works differently: it holds a hand-picked, lower-volatility stock list and gets its income from equity-linked notes, which is why it lagged in a year when the big tech names led.

One more thing: until April 30, 2025 the fund was called the Goldman Sachs S&P 500 Core Premium Income ETF, so you'll still see the old name in some places. Same fund, same ticker. On taxes, a portion of option income funds' payouts can be classified as return of capital, which lowers your cost basis instead of being taxed right away. The final split shows up on your 1099-DIV. I explained why some funds are built that way in why NEOS ETFs are tax friendly.

The Risks I'd Want You to Know

  • It's still the S&P 500. When the market drops, $GPIX drops with it. The monthly check cushions that, it doesn't prevent it. Its 52-week range was $48.46 to $56.74.
  • The payout is variable. It has trended up, but it ranged from about $0.32 to $0.40 a month since 2024, and it dipped to $0.35 in April 2026. Budget off the yearly total, not one good month.
  • Capped upside. Selling calls trims gains in big rallies. Over a full bull market, I'd expect $GPIX to trail $SPY or $VOO, even if it edged ahead this year.
  • Short track record. About three years of history, almost all of it in a strong market. Nobody knows yet how it behaves through a long bear market.

My Takeaway

$GPIX pays monthly, goes ex on the first business day, and pays about a week later. Mark Monday, November 2 as the likely next ex-date. The checks have grown every year, the share price is up, and over the last year it beat both $JEPI and $SPYI on total return while charging the lowest fee of the three.

Here's what I actually look at before buying any income ETF: does it pay reliably, has the price decayed, and what's the total return. $GPIX checks all three boxes right now. It won't give you the biggest yield in the room, and I'm fine with that. An 8% yield that keeps your principal growing beats a 12% yield that slowly eats it.
